How to (Not) Write Documentation

Explain, Don't Define

Have you ever tried to learn math off of wikipedia? My undergraduate degree is physics, and I have a math minor, but one day I hear about a "geometric algebra". Sounds interesting, and wiki's introduction confirms it "can represent and manipulate geometrical objects", but otherwise the introduction is fairly vague. I want to know how it works, and how I would use it. The next section is "Definition", and it turns out that

A unital associative algebra Cl(V, g) with a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form g : V × V → F is the Clifford algebra of the quadratic space (V, g) if:

  • it contains F and V as distinct subspaces
  • a^2 = g(a, a)1 for a ∈ V
  • V generates Cl(V, g) as an algebra
  • Cl(V, g) is not generated by any proper subspace of V.

This is apparently the version "without abstract algebra". Who does this help? I know you could start teaching geometric algebra in late primary school, at the same time as kids are learning vectors. You don't need to know what a "bilinear form" is or what makes one "nondegenerate", or what a "quadratic space" is. Who did the author think this would help? Did they only care about mathematics graduate students, or do they just not care about ordinary people? Heck, someone with a degree in math can just go look this up in a textbook or a paper, so this definition only serves as an easily-findable reference for people who already know.

Meanwhile if I want to learn how to pronounce a German "ch" sound, I'll find my way to the page about the "Voiceles Velar Fricative", and there's a picture of what I need to do. I have no formal education in phonetics, but I can still figure it out, to the point where I've learned how to produce most human speech sounds just from wikipedia.

Now let's turn back to software documentation. I've been having issues with by browser consuming a lot of memory, and I wanted to know how to limit it. Fairly quickly, I came across the command systemd-run --user --scope -p Memory Limit=1G firefox. Great, but you're not supposed to just execute commands without understanding them, so instead, I run man systemd-run.

Run programs in transient scope units, service units, or path-, socket-, or timer-triggered service units

Great. What's a "transient scope unit"? I know what a service unit is, or I thought it was a file that described a service, put apparently it's also some sort of dynamic thing? Reading up on the --user switch, there's a apparently multiple "service managers", whetever those are, and there's apparently at least one for the calling user and one for the "system" (how many systems are within arm's reach right now?).

Maybe you think I "should" know this stuff, but the fact is I don't use systemd that much, not directly. I rarely need to write service files for it. So what about an ordinary, non-programmer linux user who might want to run this command? Windows and Mac and phone users don't have to learn their (bloated) init system. They don't even need to know what an "init system" is. I don't think it's reasonable, or even professional, to go around blaming people for not having your particular expertise. I don't think it's a good thing in your life in general to take your own knowledge for granted.

I'll note that there are no obvious links or references. I know I could google it, but will I? Ordinary people will ask people for a word's definition in comments instead of just googling it. The fact of human behavior is that you cannot rely on people to learn on their own. And yet, software documentation usually doesn't even offer a hyperlink to locally-undefined technical jargon.

A good rule of thumb in technical writing is:

  • Of the people who find your thing, only 10% will read beyond the title.
  • Of the people who read past the title, only 10% will do more than a brief skim.
  • Of the people who read the whole thing, only 10% of people will dig any deeper.

The conclusion is obvious: documentation must explain itself in full by default. Do not be afraid to repeat yourself in many places. Quick snappy reference guide should not be the entrypoint, even though some people do need them. If you must assume some level of education, state those knowledge dependencies explicitly, preferably with somewhere to find a good overview, maybe even a crash-course specific to your documentation. Hyperlinks do help, but once a reader goes through just a couple, you can safely assume they've already lost the plot.

That's a high bar, and I usually don't live up to it (mostly because of time pressure). Still, it's my target, and if you're distributing software for people to actually use, it should be yours, too.
